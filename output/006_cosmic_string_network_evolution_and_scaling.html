Overview for Lay Readers

After cosmic strings form, they don't just sit there. They create a vast, tangled network stretching across the universe. This network is incredibly dynamic, like a tangled ball of rubber bands that's constantly trying to straighten itself out. The strings are under immense tension, causing them to move at nearly the speed of light and straighten any kinks. When two strings cross, they can "reconnect" or "intercommute"—slicing through each other and swapping ends. This process often pinches off small, closed loops from the larger network. These loops then oscillate, decay by emitting gravitational waves, and disappear. This simulation shows a 2D version of this process. You can watch an initial dense tangle of strings simplify itself, producing loops and evolving towards a stable, "scaling" state.

A Beginner's Guide to a Tangled Universe

Imagine you fill a large, clear box with a tangled mess of rubber bands, and then start shaking the box. What would you see? The rubber bands, which are under tension, would try to straighten themselves out. They would zip around, collide with each other, and generally thrash about. Now, imagine that whenever two rubber bands cross, they have the ability to cut each other and swap ends. A long, tangled rubber band might cross itself, and the crossing point could pinch off, creating a small, separate, closed loop of rubber band. Over time, the initial, dense chaotic mess would simplify itself, leaving a few long, relatively straight rubber bands and a growing population of small, discarded loops.

This is an excellent analogy for the evolution of a cosmic string network. After a network of cosmic strings is formed in the early universe, it doesn't just sit still. It is an incredibly dynamic system, driven by the immense tension in the strings. This tension causes two main effects: it makes the strings move at nearly the speed of light, and it acts to smooth out any kinks or wiggles, pulling the strings straight. When two strings collide, they intercommute (or reconnect), swapping partners just like our magical rubber bands. This process is the network's primary way of losing energy and complexity. It constantly chops up the long strings, creating a population of closed loops which then decay away.

This simulation shows a 2D version of this process. It starts with a random, dense network of strings. As you run the simulation, you will see the strings moving and straightening. When they cross, they will reconnect, sometimes creating new loops (shown in a different color). You are watching the universe "clean house," simplifying a complex defect network and evolving towards a stable, predictable state known as a "scaling solution." The loops produced in this process are a major potential source of gravitational waves that we might one day detect.

Core Theory: Scaling, Reconnection, and Gravitational Waves

1. The Nambu-Goto Action and String Motion

In the absence of interactions with other fields, the motion of a cosmic string is described by the Nambu-Goto action. This is a very simple and elegant principle: a string moves in such a way as to minimize the area of the two-dimensional "worldsheet" it sweeps out in four-dimensional spacetime. Minimizing this area directly implies that the string has a tension, μ, which is equal to its mass per unit length. This tension acts as a powerful restoring force, causing the string to accelerate and attempt to straighten any curve. As a result, wiggles on the string propagate as waves along the string at the speed of light, and the strings themselves can move at relativistic speeds.

2. Intercommutation: The Key Interaction

The most crucial process in network evolution is intercommutation (or reconnection). When two strings cross, they can break at the intersection point and swap partners. If a string ABCD crosses a string EFGH, they can become ABGH and EFCD. The probability of this happening is generally assumed to be close to 1. This process is the engine of network simplification. It allows the tangled network to "untangle" itself by creating smaller, simpler structures. A particularly important form of intercommutation is when a string intersects with itself. This can "pinch off" a segment, creating a closed loop of string that is disconnected from the main, long string network.

3. The Scaling Solution

A remarkable feature of string network evolution is that it is expected to reach a scaling solution. This is a state where the statistical properties of the network remain constant when viewed in terms of the cosmic horizon size. The universe's expansion constantly stretches the network, trying to decrease its density. At the same time, the string dynamics—straightening and intercommutation—are constantly chopping up the network and reducing its length. A stable equilibrium is reached between these two competing effects. The network settles into a self-similar configuration where there are always a few long strings stretching across the horizon, plus a distribution of smaller loops. This means that the energy density of the string network always remains a tiny, constant fraction of the total energy density of the universe at any given epoch. This prevents strings from dominating the universe and makes them a viable cosmological possibility.

4. Loop Production and Gravitational Wave Emission

The pinched-off loops are the network's primary method of energy loss. Once formed, these closed loops oscillate relativistically under their own tension. An oscillating, massive loop is a powerful source of gravitational waves. The loops radiate away their energy into these ripples in spacetime, causing them to shrink and eventually disappear completely. Since strings would have been forming and producing loops throughout the entire history of the universe, this process would create a stochastic gravitational wave background—a persistent hum of gravitational waves coming from all directions. Searching for this specific background signal is one of the main goals of gravitational wave observatories like LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA, and the future space-based observatory LISA.

Deep Q&A (25+ Questions)

  1. What is a "scaling solution" and why is it so important?

    A scaling solution is a state where the large-scale properties of the network look statistically the same at all times, relative to the scale of the universe (the horizon). This means the number of long strings per horizon volume, and the overall string density relative to the background energy density, remain constant. This is critically important because without it, the string energy density would decrease more slowly than matter and radiation, and strings would eventually dominate the energy of the universe, which is contrary to observation. Scaling provides a natural mechanism for strings to avoid this fate.

  2. What happens to the loops after they are produced?

    They decay. A closed loop of string will oscillate under its own tension, and any accelerating mass emits gravitational radiation. These loops radiate their energy away in the form of gravitational waves, causing them to shrink and eventually vanish in a final burst of high-energy particles. The lifetime of a loop is proportional to its initial size.

  3. How can we detect a cosmic string network?

    The most promising method is by detecting the stochastic gravitational wave background produced by the decaying loops. Different string tensions (Gμ) and loop sizes predict different spectra (the amplitude of the signal at different frequencies). Pulsar Timing Arrays are sensitive to very low-frequency waves from supermassive loops, while ground-based detectors like LIGO are sensitive to higher frequencies from smaller loops. A detection would not only prove the existence of strings but also provide a window into the physics of the very early universe.

  4. What is the Nambu-Goto action?

    It's the simplest possible action for a one-dimensional object (a string) moving in spacetime. It states that the string's dynamics are governed by the principle of minimizing its own worldsheet area. It's the string equivalent of the principle that a point particle travels along a geodesic to minimize its path length. It beautifully encapsulates the idea that a string's dynamics are driven entirely by its own tension.

  5. In this 2D simulation, what do the string "dynamics" actually do?

    This simulation uses a simplified "kinematic" model. In each step: 1. A force is calculated for each point (vertex) on a string, based on the string's curvature at that point. This force represents the string tension trying to straighten the string. 2. The point's position is updated based on this force, causing the string to move and smooth out. 3. The code checks for intersections between all the line segments making up the strings. 4. If an intersection is found, the reconnection algorithm is triggered, splitting and rejoining the strings. This may create a new, separate loop.

  6. What is the "reconnection probability"?

    This is the probability that two strings will actually intercommute when they cross. While classical strings always reconnect, quantum effects might prevent it some of the time. In most simulations, this probability is assumed to be 1, but some models explore lower values to see how it affects the network's evolution. A lower probability would lead to a denser, more tangled network as it would be less efficient at simplifying itself.

  7. What are cusps and kinks on a string loop?

    These are special points on an oscillating string loop that are particularly important for gravitational wave emission. A kink is a sharp corner on a string, typically formed during a reconnection event, which travels along the string at the speed of light. A cusp is a point on a smooth loop where the string momentarily reaches the speed of light. These cusps and kinks produce powerful, beamed bursts of gravitational radiation, which could potentially be detected as individual events, not just part of the background hum.

  8. Why does the network look "the same" over time?

    This is the visual manifestation of the scaling solution. As the simulation runs, you see strings reconnecting and creating loops, which reduces the total length of the "long string" network. If the box were expanding (which is not shown in this simulation for simplicity), the strings would also be stretched apart. The scaling solution is the equilibrium where the rate of string length loss due to loop production exactly balances the rate of string length increase due to cosmic expansion. The result is that the number of long strings crossing a box of a given size remains roughly constant.

  9. How does this relate to the cosmic microwave background (CMB)?

    While the primary signature of a string network is gravitational waves, the strings themselves would also leave faint imprints on the CMB. The Kaiser-Stebbins effect, as discussed in another simulation, describes how a moving string creates a line-like temperature discontinuity. A whole network of strings would create a complex web of these small discontinuities, contributing to the overall pattern of temperature fluctuations in the CMB. Current CMB data from the Planck satellite has been used to place some of the strongest constraints on the tension of cosmic strings.

  10. Are the loops all the same size?

    No, a key prediction of network simulations is that they produce a broad distribution of loop sizes. The exact distribution depends on the details of the model, but typically, many more small loops are produced than large ones. The size of the loop is critical as it determines the frequency and amplitude of the gravitational waves it emits. Summing up the contributions from all these different loop sizes across cosmic history is how the final stochastic background is calculated.

  11. What is the difference between "long strings" and "loops"?

    In simulations, "long strings" are those whose length is comparable to or larger than the simulation box (or horizon size). They are effectively infinite. "Loops" are closed strings that are much smaller than the horizon. The network dynamics are a process of converting energy from long strings into loops, which then decay.

  12. What is the "Velocity-dependent One-Scale" (VOS) model?

    The VOS model is a powerful analytic tool for studying network evolution. Instead of simulating individual strings, it tracks two key macroscopic quantities: the average length of string per unit volume (L) and the average velocity of the strings (v). It uses a set of coupled differential equations to describe how L and v change over time due to cosmic expansion, string tension, and loop production. It's a much faster way to get large-scale predictions than full numerical simulations.

  13. What is "backreaction"?

    Backreaction refers to the effect of small-scale wiggles on the large-scale motion of a string. A string isn't perfectly smooth; it's covered in tiny wiggles left over from its formation and from reconnection events. These wiggles act as a kind of "friction" or drag, slowing down the large-scale straightening of the string and making its evolution more complex than the simple Nambu-Goto action would suggest. Modern simulations try to account for this.

  14. Why is this a 2D simulation and not 3D?

    A full 3D simulation of a string network is computationally very expensive. It requires checking for intersections between thousands of line segments in 3D space at every time step. A 2D simulation captures the essential physics—straightening, intersection, and reconnection—in a way that is much more suitable for an interactive web browser demonstration, while still illustrating the core concepts of network simplification and loop production.

  15. What does the "Loop Size Distribution" chart show?

    This chart is a histogram that shows how many loops of different sizes have been produced by the network. You will typically see that the network produces many small loops and progressively fewer large loops. This distribution is a key prediction of string network models and is crucial for calculating the expected gravitational wave signal.
