Overview for the Curious

Why does a frisbee fly stably, but a tennis racket flipped in the air starts to tumble chaotically? Why do planets have a slight wobble in their rotation? The answers lie in the physics of **rigid body rotation**. Unlike a simple point particle, a real object has a shape and a size, described by its three **principal moments of inertia**. This simulation lets you define an object's shape by setting these three numbers and then give it a spin. You'll see how the **angular velocity vector (ω)** and the **angular momentum vector (L)** dance around each other, creating beautiful patterns of motion like **precession**. Most strikingly, you can test the famous **Intermediate Axis Theorem**, which shows that rotation around an object's longest and shortest axes is stable, but rotation around its middle axis is spectacularly unstable.

The Tennis Racket Theorem

One of the most famous and unintuitive results in classical mechanics is the intermediate axis theorem. It states that for any object with three different principal moments of inertia (like a book, a phone, or a tennis racket), rotation about the axes with the largest and smallest moments of inertia is stable. If you spin the object around one of these axes, it will continue to spin smoothly. However, rotation about the axis with the *intermediate* moment of inertia is unstable. If you try to spin the object perfectly around this middle axis, any tiny perturbation will cause it to begin tumbling chaotically, flipping over periodically. This phenomenon is also known as the Dzhanibekov effect, after the Soviet cosmonaut who observed it with a wingnut in zero gravity. This simulation allows you to witness this instability firsthand and understand the beautiful mathematics of Euler's equations that govern it.
