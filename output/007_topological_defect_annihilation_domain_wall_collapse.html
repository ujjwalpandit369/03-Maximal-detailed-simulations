Overview for Lay Readers

Topological defects are not necessarily permanent. If a defect forms a closed loop, like a circle of cosmic string or a bubble of domain wall, it is typically unstable. The tension in the defect acts like a stretched rubber band, pulling the loop inward and causing it to shrink. As it shrinks, it accelerates, converting its massive energy into kinetic energy and eventually releasing it in a burst of particles and radiation when it collapses to a point. This simulation shows this process for a domain wall. We start with a circular "bubble" of one vacuum state inside another. When you run the simulation, you will see the tension in the wall cause the bubble to shrink and ultimately annihilate, disappearing from the grid.

A Beginner's Guide to Cosmic Disappearances

Imagine blowing a soap bubble. It forms a beautiful, spherical film. But what happens if it doesn't pop? The surface tension of the soap film, the same force that pulls it into a sphere, constantly tries to shrink the bubble's surface area. If there were no air trapped inside, this tension would cause the bubble to rapidly shrink until it vanished into a tiny droplet. This shrinking and disappearing act is a wonderful analogy for defect annihilation. Cosmic defects, like domain walls and strings, are not just static objects; they are under immense tension.

If a defect forms a closed loop—like a bubble-shaped domain wall or a rubber-band-shaped cosmic string—it is generally not stable. The tension acts like a cosmic rubber band, pulling the loop inward. This causes the loop to shrink, accelerating as it goes, moving at nearly the speed of light. As it shrinks, its enormous stored energy (its mass) is converted into kinetic energy. Finally, the loop collapses to a single point and annihilates, releasing all its energy in a spectacular burst of fundamental particles and radiation. This process is fundamental to cosmology; it's the universe's primary way of cleaning up the debris left over from cosmic phase transitions.

This simulation focuses on this single, crucial process. We don't start with a random mess as in the first simulation. Instead, we begin with a pre-made, circular "bubble" of one vacuum state inside another, creating a single, closed domain wall. When you run the simulation, you are not watching defects form; you are watching one die. You will see the wall's tension pull it inward, causing the circular domain to shrink and, finally, annihilate completely, leaving behind a uniform, empty space.

Core Theory: The Dynamics of Collapse

1. Tension as a Driving Force

The key to understanding annihilation is the concept of tension. For a domain wall, this is the surface tension, σ (energy per unit area). For a cosmic string, it is the line tension, μ (energy per unit length). This tension is not just a bookkeeping device for energy; it is a real physical force. Just as the tension in a stretched guitar string pulls it back to a straight line, the tension in a cosmic defect tries to minimize its extent.

For any curved segment of a defect, the tension results in a net force pointing towards the center of curvature. The magnitude of this force is proportional to the tension and the curvature. For a circular domain wall loop of radius R, the curvature is 1/R. This results in an inward pressure on the entire wall, given by P = σ/R. This pressure is what drives the collapse. As the radius R gets smaller, the pressure gets larger, causing the wall to accelerate inwards ever faster.

2. Equation of Motion for a Collapsing Wall

We can model the collapse more formally. The acceleration 'a' of a piece of the wall is given by Newton's second law, F=ma. The force is related to the pressure P, and the mass is related to the wall's energy density σ. This leads to a simplified equation of motion for the radius R of the loop: d²R/dt² ≈ - (1/R) * (c²/γ²) where 'c' is the speed of light and 'γ' is the Lorentz factor, accounting for relativistic effects as the wall approaches 'c'. The key feature is the -1/R term, which shows that the inward acceleration becomes extremely large as the radius approaches zero. The simulation you are watching is a numerical solution of a more complex version of this dynamic, showing how the Ising model field evolves under the imperative to minimize the length (and thus energy) of the high-energy wall.

3. Annihilation Products: Where Does the Energy Go?

A topological defect is a concentration of the energy of a scalar field. When a loop collapses and annihilates, this energy must be conserved. It is converted into a burst of real, propagating particles. Specifically, the energy is released as quanta of the scalar field that originally formed the defect. These particles, however, are typically unstable and quickly decay into a shower of more familiar Standard Model particles, such as photons, electrons, quarks, and gluons. The final result of a defect annihilation event is a localized, energetic burst of ordinary matter and radiation. The total energy of these particles is exactly equal to the initial energy (mass) of the defect loop.

4. Comparing Annihilation Processes

  • Domain Wall Loops: Collapse due to surface tension. The inward pressure (σ/R) drives the collapse.
  • String Loops: Collapse due to line tension. The inward force is proportional to μ/R. String loops also develop high-frequency oscillations ("wiggles") which are radiated away as gravitational waves, providing another energy loss mechanism.
  • Monopole-Antimonopole Pairs: These are point-like particles with opposite "magnetic charges." They will attract each other via a magnetic Coulomb-like force and accelerate towards each other until they collide and annihilate, releasing their enormous mass energy.

While the specific mechanisms differ, the end result is the same: the conversion of the defect's potential and gradient energy into radiation and relativistic particles.

Deep Q&A (20+ Questions)

  1. Why does a closed loop of a defect always collapse?

    Because it is energetically favorable. The defect represents a state of high energy compared to the true vacuum. The total energy of a loop is proportional to its size (length for a string, area for a wall). By shrinking, the loop can reduce its total energy. Since physical systems always seek to minimize their energy, the loop will inevitably shrink if there is a continuous path to a lower energy state. For a simple closed loop, there is no energy barrier preventing this collapse.

  2. Does the collapse happen at the speed of light?

    It approaches the speed of light. As the loop shrinks, the forces increase, causing it to accelerate. Relativistic effects become important. The mass of the loop increases due to its kinetic energy (via the Lorentz factor γ), which prevents it from ever quite reaching the speed of light. However, in its final moments, it will be moving at a velocity indistinguishable from 'c'.

  3. What is "tension" in this context? Is it like a rubber band?

    The analogy is very good. The tension of a defect (σ for a wall, μ for a string) is dimensionally an energy per unit area or length. But because of relativity (E=mc²), this is also a mass per unit area/length. And because pressure and energy density have the same units, it also acts as a pressure. A domain wall has a positive energy density but a negative pressure. This negative pressure is what creates the effective "tension" that pulls the wall inward.

  4. Could we see the flash of light from a defect annihilating?

    If a defect were to annihilate nearby, the burst of high-energy photons would be spectacular and easily detectable. However, since we expect these events to have happened primarily in the very early universe, the light from them would be incredibly redshifted by the cosmic expansion. It would likely arrive today as a faint signal in the radio or microwave part of the spectrum. Searching for such transient events is one possible way to look for evidence of defects.

  5. What happens if two domain walls collide head-on?

    They will annihilate. Imagine two flat sheets moving towards each other. When they meet, the region of space between them has been "eaten." The two walls merge and their combined energy is released along the collision plane. The same happens for a string and an anti-string, or a monopole and an anti-monopole.

  6. In the simulation, why does the wall seem to get "bumpy" as it shrinks?

    This is a realistic effect due to the discrete nature of the simulation grid and thermal fluctuations. The underlying algorithm (Ising model evolution) involves random spin flips based on energy changes. This introduces noise. As the wall shrinks, small, random perturbations can grow into "wiggles" on the wall's surface. A perfectly smooth circle would only shrink, but these wiggles can propagate as waves along the wall, leading to a more complex and physically realistic collapse.

  7. What do the metrics "Wall Length" and "Domain Area" show?

    These metrics quantify the collapse.

    • Domain Area: This is simply a count of the number of spins inside the loop. You will see this number decrease steadily as the loop shrinks.
    • Wall Length: This is an estimate of the length of the boundary between the two domains. It's calculated by counting the number of neighboring pairs of spins that are misaligned. This value should also decrease as the loop shrinks and becomes smoother before vanishing.
    Plotting these against time provides a clear graph of the annihilation event.

  8. How does the "Coupling J" parameter affect the collapse?

    The coupling constant J sets the energy scale of the simulation. It is directly proportional to the wall's tension, σ. A higher value of J means a higher tension. Therefore, increasing J will cause the domain wall loop to collapse much more quickly, as the inward-pulling force will be stronger. Decreasing J will lead to a slower, more leisurely collapse.

  9. What is the role of Temperature T?

    Temperature introduces random thermal fluctuations. At very low T, the collapse will be smooth and driven purely by the wall tension. As you increase T, random spin flips will make the wall "jiggle" more. This thermal noise can slightly slow down or speed up the collapse, and makes the process less deterministic. At very high temperatures (above the critical temperature of the phase transition), the thermal energy would be so great that it would overwhelm the wall tension, and the loop would simply dissolve into a random sea of spins instead of collapsing coherently.

  10. Is it possible for a defect loop to be stable?

    Yes, under certain circumstances. While a simple loop is unstable, a loop could be stabilized if it carries a conserved quantity. For example, some theories predict "superconducting" cosmic strings that can carry a persistent current. The angular momentum of this current can act like a gyroscope, creating an outward centrifugal force that can balance the inward pull of the tension, leading to a stable, spinning loop called a "vorton." These are candidates for dark matter.
