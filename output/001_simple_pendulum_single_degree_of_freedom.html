    Overview for the Curious

    Imagine a weight swinging back and forth at the end of a string. That's a simple pendulum. This simulation lets you play with that fundamental concept. A "degree of freedom" is just a fancy term for a way something can move. For our simple pendulum, its only way to move is by changing its angle. It can't get longer, shorter, or move sideways (in this idealized model). By exploring this single degree of freedom, we can uncover surprisingly complex behaviors, like how the swing time (period) changes with gravity or length, and witness the beautiful, unwavering law of conservation of energy.

    Beginner's Guide to the Pendulum

    The simple pendulum is one of the first things students often study in physics, and for good reason. It's a perfect example of an oscillator—a system that moves back and forth around a stable equilibrium point. Here, the equilibrium is the pendulum hanging straight down, motionless. When you pull it to the side and release it, gravity pulls it back towards the center. But by the time it reaches the center, it has some speed, so it overshoots, swinging up the other side. Gravity then slows it down, stops it, and pulls it back again. This repeats, creating the familiar swinging motion.

    The key property we care about is the period (T): the time it takes for one full swing (back and forth). For small angles (less than about 15°), this period depends almost exclusively on two things: the length of the string (L) and the strength of gravity (g). It does not depend on the mass of the bob or the initial angle (for small angles). This is the magic of the pendulum and why it was used for centuries in clocks!

    Core Theory

    The motion of the simple pendulum is governed by a differential equation derived from Newton's second law for rotation: Στ = Iα. The only torque (τ) acting on the pendulum bob (relative to the pivot) is from the component of gravity perpendicular to the string. This torque is τ = -mgL sin(θ), where 'm' is mass, 'g' is gravity, 'L' is length, and 'θ' is the angle from the vertical. The negative sign indicates it's a restoring torque, always pulling the pendulum back to center (θ=0).

    The moment of inertia (I) of a point mass is I = mL², and the angular acceleration is α = d²θ/dt². Plugging these in gives: -mgL sin(θ) = (mL²) d²θ/dt². Simplifying, we get the non-linear second-order differential equation:

    d²θ/dt² + (g/L) sin(θ) = 0

    This equation is difficult to solve analytically. However, for small angles, we can use the approximation sin(θ) ≈ θ (where θ is in radians). This simplifies the equation to that of a simple harmonic oscillator:

    d²θ/dt² + (g/L) θ = 0

    The solution to this is θ(t) = θmax cos(ωt + φ), where ω = √(g/L) is the angular frequency. The period T is 2π/ω, which gives the famous formula for a simple pendulum's period: T ≈ 2π √(L/g).
