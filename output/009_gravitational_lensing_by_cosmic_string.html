Overview for Lay Readers

According to Einstein, massive objects warp spacetime, and light follows these warps. This is called gravitational lensing. A cosmic string, being incredibly massive, also acts as a gravitational lens, but in a very strange way. Instead of pulling things towards it, the spacetime around a string is "conical"—like a cone's surface. This means that as light from a distant galaxy passes the string, it can travel along two different paths to reach us. The result is that we see two identical, undistorted images of the same galaxy, separated by a small angle. This simulation lets you be the lens. A Beginner's Guide to Cosmic Mirages

We're all familiar with how a lens, like a magnifying glass, can bend light to make things look bigger. In space, gravity can do the same thing. According to Einstein's theory of General Relativity, a massive object like a galaxy or a black hole can warp the fabric of spacetime around it. Light from a more distant object has to follow this warped path. This bending of light by gravity is called gravitational lensing. It can magnify, distort, and even create multiple images of a single background object. It's like looking through the warped, thick glass of an old bottle.

A cosmic string, being unimaginably dense, is also a powerful gravitational lens, but it creates a mirage with a very particular, ghostly signature. Unlike a galaxy, which pulls spacetime inward like a bowling ball on a trampoline, the spacetime around a long, straight string is "conical." Imagine taking a flat sheet of paper, cutting out a thin wedge, and taping the edges together to make a cone. The surface of the cone is still flat everywhere locally (a tiny ant walking on it wouldn't notice any curvature), but the overall geometry is different. Light from a distant quasar that passes behind this "cone" can now take two different paths to reach our eyes—one path going around the left side of the cone, and one going around the right.

The astonishing result is that we would see two identical, completely undistorted images of the same quasar in the sky, sitting right next to each other. This is the unique smoking-gun signature of a cosmic string lens. This simulation allows you to be the astronomer. You have a field of distant "galaxies," and you can place a cosmic string in front of them. By moving the string, you can see how it creates these perfect double images for any galaxy that falls behind it, a unique and powerful probe into the fundamental physics of the early universe.

Core Theory: Conical Spacetime and Deflection Angles

1. Gravitational Lensing by Standard Matter

To appreciate the strangeness of string lensing, we first review standard lensing. A massive object like a galaxy cluster creates a "gravitational potential well." Light rays passing near it are bent inwards. This can lead to several observable effects:

  • Magnification: The lensed object appears brighter.
  • Distortion (Shear): A circular galaxy can be stretched into a long, thin arc.
  • Multiple Images: For strong alignment, the light can be split into multiple (often four, in a configuration called an "Einstein Cross") images around the lensing object.
These effects depend strongly on the mass of the lensing galaxy and the precise alignment of the source, lens, and observer.

2. The Conical Spacetime Metric

The spacetime around an idealized, straight cosmic string is fundamentally different. There is no gravitational attraction towards the string. The metric describing this spacetime in cylindrical coordinates (r, φ, z) is: ds² = dt² - dz² - dr² - r²(1 - 4Gμ)² dφ² Here, G is the gravitational constant and μ is the string's mass per unit length (its tension). The crucial part is the term (1 - 4Gμ)² multiplying the angular coordinate dφ². This means that as you go around the string in a circle, the total angle is not 360 degrees (2π radians), but slightly less: 2π * (1 - 4Gμ). The missing angle, δ = 8πGμ, is called the deficit angle. Spacetime is flat, but it has a wedge "cut out" of it, with the string sitting at the seam.

3. Deriving the Double Image

Imagine a light source (a quasar, Q) is located behind the string from our perspective (the observer, O). A light ray can travel from Q to O on a straight path. However, because of the conical geometry, a second path exists. A ray can travel on the "other side" of the cone's seam. To the observer, these two rays appear to come from slightly different directions in the sky. The observer sees two images, Q1 and Q2. Because the spacetime is locally flat, the light rays are not bent or distorted. As a result, the two images are identical to the source object—they are not magnified or sheared. They are simply displaced.

4. The Deflection Angle

The angular separation between the two images (Δθ) is exactly equal to the deficit angle created by the string: Δθ = δ = 8πGμ This is a remarkable formula. The separation angle depends *only* on the fundamental physics of the string (its tension, Gμ) and nothing else. It does not depend on the distance to the string, or the distance to the background galaxy, or how close the galaxy is to the string (as long as it's behind it). This provides a "standard ruler" on the sky. If we were to find a pair of identical, undistorted images, we could measure their separation and directly calculate the tension of the cosmic string, giving us a direct window into physics at ultra-high energy scales.

Deep Q&A (20+ Questions)

  1. Why are the lensed images from a string not distorted or magnified?

    Because the spacetime around the string is locally flat. In standard lensing, the curvature of spacetime (the "gravitational pull") is what stretches and magnifies the image, an effect called tidal shear. The conical spacetime of a string has no local curvature. Light rays travel in perfect straight lines. The double image is purely a topological effect, arising from the fact that there are two distinct "straight-line" paths from the source to the observer around the cone.

  2. Have we ever found a cosmic string lens?

    No, despite extensive searches. Several candidate systems have been identified over the years, where two very similar images of a quasar or galaxy are seen close together. The most famous was the "CSL-1" candidate. However, upon closer inspection with more powerful telescopes like the Hubble Space Telescope, subtle differences between the images were always found, or a massive galaxy was discovered hiding between them, revealing them to be standard gravitational lenses, not string lenses. The lack of detection is another key constraint on string abundance and tension.

  3. How large is the expected separation angle?

    Using the upper limit on string tension from CMB observations (Gμ ≲ 10⁻⁷), the maximum expected separation angle is Δθ ≈ 8π * 10⁻⁷ radians, which is about 0.4 arcseconds. This is a very small angle, but it is well within the resolving power of modern telescopes like Hubble or the James Webb Space Telescope, which is why searches are feasible.

  4. In the simulation, why do the double images appear as soon as the galaxy is behind the string?

    This demonstrates the key principle. For a theoretically infinite, straight string, any source behind the line defined by the string will be lensed. The two images are created, and their separation is constant, regardless of where the source is behind the string. Our simulation idealizes this by showing the effect for any part of the galaxy's "body" that crosses the line.

  5. What does the "String Tension (Gμ)" slider control in this simulation?

    This slider directly controls the value of Gμ used in the lensing calculation. As you increase the tension, the deficit angle δ = 8πGμ increases. This will cause the separation between the two lensed images to grow larger in real-time on the screen. This allows you to directly visualize the core prediction of string lensing theory.

  6. What if the string is not perfectly straight?

    If the string is a curved loop, the lensing signature becomes much more complex. It can produce more than two images, and the images can become distorted and magnified, making them harder to distinguish from standard lensing by a galaxy. However, even for a loop, there are specific predictions for the image patterns and properties that would differ from a standard lens.

  7. Does the lensing effect depend on the string's motion?

    No, the lensing effect itself does not. The double-image geometry is due to the static conical spacetime. The string's motion is responsible for a different phenomenon, the Kaiser-Stebbins effect on the CMB. The two are distinct signatures of the same underlying object.

  8. Why is this a "smoking gun" signature?

    Because the predicted signal is so clean and unique. Finding a pair of images that are (a) identical in brightness and shape, (b) undistorted, and (c) have no visible lensing galaxy between them would be extremely difficult to explain with any conventional astrophysical object. It would almost have to be a cosmic string.

  9. How does the user interaction work here?

    The simulation allows you to click and drag the mouse to position the cosmic string on the 2D canvas. The lensing calculation is performed in real-time. The code continuously checks which of the background galaxies are behind the current position of the string line and updates the display to show either the single original galaxy or its two lensed copies.

  10. Could other defects cause gravitational lensing?

    Yes, but with different signatures. A domain wall would cause light to be repelled from it, shifting the apparent position of objects. Monopoles are point masses and would produce standard (Schwarzschild) lensing, but their predicted mass is generally too low to produce a detectable lensing signal on their own.
